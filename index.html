The foundations of society

Why do we need government?

Think about what would happen if nobody organised public services, settled disputes or protected people's rights. A government helps a society work together instead of leaving every problem unresolved.

01 / ORDER

Maintain peace

Laws establish rules for public life. Police, courts and other institutions help enforce those laws and deal with wrongdoing. Fair rules should apply to everyone.

02 / SERVICES

Provide public goods

Governments help organise roads, public transport, schools, sanitation, hospitals and disaster relief. These services support entire communities.

03 / RIGHTS

Protect people

Constitutions and laws can protect freedoms, promote equality and prevent unfair treatment. Independent courts can help people challenge abuses of power.

04 / JUSTICE

Resolve conflicts

Disagreements over land, contracts, public resources or rights need peaceful solutions. Courts and other public institutions provide ways to settle disputes.

Compare and understand

Five systems, five different answers

The easiest way to compare governments is to ask three questions: Who has power? Can ordinary citizens participate? What guides the making of laws?

Government Who holds power? Citizen participation Basis of lawmaking Example
Democracy Citizens directly or through elected representatives Voting, public debate and other lawful participation Constitution, legislation and public institutions India
Monarchy A king, queen or other monarch; actual power varies Depends on whether the monarchy is constitutional or absolute Constitution and laws, or the monarch's authority in an absolute system United Kingdom, a constitutional monarchy
Dictatorship A single ruler or a tightly controlling regime Often heavily restricted, especially in political opposition Ruler's decisions, decrees and institutions under the regime Chile under Augusto Pinochet
Theocracy Religious authorities or a system claiming religious authority Varies; religious and political participation may be restricted Religious doctrine alongside the state's legal framework Iran, an Islamic republic with powerful religious institutions
Oligarchy A small, powerful group, often based on wealth, family or military influence May be limited, especially if elites dominate decisions Decisions shaped by the interests and institutions of the ruling group Ancient Sparta's narrow ruling citizen class

Important: real countries do not always fit neatly into one box. Their political systems can combine features of several categories. These are simplified classroom comparisons, not complete descriptions of every country's laws or institutions.
